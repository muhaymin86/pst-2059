LESSON 01 / THE BASICS

Before geography: understand maps

Before locating Pakistan or describing its mountains, you need to understand how geographers describe where places are.

1. What is geography?

Geography is the study of the Earth, its physical features, its people and the relationship between people and the environment. Physical geography studies natural features such as mountains, rivers and climate. Human geography studies people, settlements, transport and economic activities.

Pakistan Studies Paper 2 connects both. For example, a mountain is a physical feature, but the way a steep mountain slope affects road construction is a human and economic issue.

2. What is a map?

A map is a scaled, flat representation of all or part of the Earth's surface. A map allows us to identify places, measure distances, compare directions and recognise physical features.

Scale

Scale compares a distance on a map with the actual distance on the ground. For example, a scale of 1:100,000 means 1 cm on the map represents 100,000 cm on the ground, which equals 1 km.

Direction

Direction tells you where one place is in relation to another. The four main compass directions are north, east, south and west. Intermediate directions include northeast, northwest, southeast and southwest.

Legend / key

A map key explains what symbols, colours and lines represent. A blue line may represent a river, while contour lines may represent changes in elevation.

North arrow

A north arrow shows the direction of north. It helps you identify the other compass directions and describe the relative position of places.

3. What are latitude and longitude?

Latitude and longitude form an imaginary grid around the Earth. This grid allows us to describe the location of any place using numbers measured in degrees.

Latitude — north or south

Latitude measures how far a place is north or south of the Equator. Lines of latitude run from west to east around the Earth.

The Equator is 0° latitude. Locations north of it are written with N, while locations south of it are written with S.

Lines of latitude are also called parallels because they remain parallel to the Equator and do not meet.

Longitude — east or west

Longitude measures how far a place is east or west of the Prime Meridian, which is 0° longitude and passes through Greenwich in the United Kingdom.

Lines of longitude run from the North Pole to the South Pole. They are also called meridians and meet at the poles.

Longitudes are written with E or W.

NORTH POLE 90°N | WEST -------- EQUATOR -------- EAST 0° | SOUTH POLE 90°S LATITUDE: north or south of Equator LONGITUDE: east or west of Prime Meridian
Worked location example

A place at 34°N, 73°E is north of the Equator and east of the Prime Meridian.

Read the latitude first, then the longitude. The letters N, S, E and W tell you which side of the reference line the place is on.

4. Important lines of latitude

The Equator divides the Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The Tropic of Cancer is approximately 23.5°N. It marks the northernmost latitude where the Sun can be directly overhead at some point in the year.

Pakistan is close to the Tropic of Cancer. It is important to recognise the line on the syllabus map rather than assume it marks Pakistan's border.

90°N — North Pole 66.5°N — Arctic Circle 23.5°N — Tropic of Cancer 0° — Equator 23.5°S — Tropic of Capricorn 66.5°S — Antarctic Circle 90°S — South Pole

5. Why do coordinates matter?

Coordinates let us describe a location accurately instead of saying something vague such as "somewhere in the north". They also help us compare the position of places and identify patterns across a country.

For example, the syllabus asks you to identify 30°N and 36°N, as well as 64°E, 70°E and 76°E, on a map of Pakistan. You should practise recognising these lines on the actual map in your atlas.

Remember this distinction

Latitude is north–south position. Longitude is east–west position. Latitude is particularly important for explaining differences in the angle of the Sun and seasonal day length. Longitude is closely related to local solar time. Longitude by itself does not determine a place's climate.

LESSON 02 / LOCATION

Where is Pakistan?

Pakistan is located in South Asia. Its position connects the South Asian region with neighbouring areas of Central Asia and the Middle East.

1. Pakistan's geographical position

Geographical location means the position of a place on the Earth's surface. It can be described using coordinates, neighbouring countries and nearby physical features.

Pakistan occupies the northwestern part of South Asia. It has land borders with India, China, Afghanistan and Iran. To the south lies the Arabian Sea, which forms part of the northern Indian Ocean.

2. The Arabian Sea

The Arabian Sea is part of the north-western Indian Ocean. It lies to the south of Pakistan and borders the coastal areas of Sindh and Balochistan.

Its importance includes sea transport, fishing, ports, international trade and coastal livelihoods. Karachi and Gwadar are important coastal cities. Ports allow goods to be moved between Pakistan and overseas markets.

3. Why is Pakistan's location important?

Trade and access to the sea

Pakistan has a coastline and ports on the Arabian Sea. These provide access to international shipping routes and allow imported and exported goods to move by sea.

Connection with Central Asia

Pakistan is near Central Asian countries. Routes through Afghanistan and the mountain passes can potentially connect Pakistan with inland areas, although difficult terrain and political conditions can limit transport.

Connection with China

Pakistan shares a border with China in the north-east. Mountain routes and transport links provide a connection between the two countries, although the high terrain makes construction and maintenance difficult.

Climate and natural environment

Pakistan's latitude, distance from the sea, elevation and position relative to mountain ranges all help explain its varied temperatures and rainfall.

Do not overstate the strategic advantages

A location near other regions does not automatically make trade easy. Mountainous terrain, transport costs, infrastructure, border arrangements and political conditions affect whether routes are practical.

An administrative area is a part of a country that has its own system of government or administration. Countries divide territory into smaller areas so that public services, law, planning and government can be organised.

Pakistan has four provinces, as well as the Islamabad Capital Territory, Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir, which have distinct administrative arrangements.

LESSON 04 / PHYSICAL GEOGRAPHY

Natural topography of Pakistan

Topography describes the shape and physical features of the land. Pakistan contains high mountains, plateaux, plains, river valleys and deserts.

1. Understand the vocabulary first

Topography

The arrangement and shape of natural and physical features on the Earth's surface. It includes the height, slope and form of the land.

Relief

The differences in height and shape of the land in an area. A region with high peaks, deep valleys and steep slopes has high relief.

Elevation

The height of a place above mean sea level. Elevation is usually measured in metres.

Gradient

How steep or gentle a slope is. A steep gradient means height changes rapidly over a short horizontal distance.

Mountain range

A chain or group of connected mountains. Several individual peaks and valleys may form part of one range.

Plateau

An area of elevated land with a relatively flat or gently rolling upper surface. A plateau is higher than the surrounding lowlands.

Plain

A large area of generally flat or gently undulating land. Plains are often suitable for settlement, farming and transport.

Valley

A lower area between hills or mountains, often containing a river or stream. Settlements and roads may follow valley floors.

2. The main physical regions

For learning purposes, Pakistan's landscape can be grouped into the northern mountains, western mountains, Potwar Plateau and Salt Range, Balochistan Plateau, Indus Plain and desert regions.

NORTH HINDU KUSH / KARAKORAM / HIMALAYA | WESTERN MOUNTAINS | POTWAR PLATEAU + SALT RANGE | UPPER INDUS PLAIN | LOWER INDUS PLAIN | ARABIAN SEA SOUTH BALOCHISTAN PLATEAU lies mainly to the west.

3. The northern mountains

The northern part of Pakistan contains some of the highest mountain systems in the world. Three important named ranges are the Hindu Kush, Karakoram and Himalayas.

Hindu Kush

The Hindu Kush lies mainly in the north-western mountain region. In Pakistan it is closely associated with Chitral and surrounding highlands. The terrain includes high peaks, narrow valleys and steep slopes.

These conditions can make road construction, farming and settlement difficult.

Karakoram

The Karakoram is in the far north, especially around Gilgit-Baltistan. It contains extremely high peaks, glaciers and rugged valleys.

K2, at 8,611 metres, is the world's second-highest mountain and is part of the Karakoram range.

Himalayas

The Himalayas extend across the north and north-east. Pakistan's Himalayan region includes steep slopes, deep valleys and high peaks.

Nanga Parbat, at about 8,126 metres, is a major peak in the western Himalayas.

What do the northern mountains look like?

They commonly have high, pointed peaks, deep valleys, steep slopes and narrow river gorges. At high elevations, temperatures are low and snow and ice may remain for long periods.

A glacier is a large body of ice that moves slowly under its own weight. Glaciers store freshwater and release meltwater into rivers, particularly during warmer months.

4. The western mountains

The western mountain region includes several rugged ranges and hill systems. These mountains are important physical barriers between the Indus Plain and areas to the west and north-west.

Safed Koh

Safed Koh, also known as Koh-i-Sufaid, is a mountain range near the Pakistan–Afghanistan frontier in the north-west. Its mountainous terrain includes steep slopes and valleys.

Sulaiman Range

The Sulaiman Range lies west of the Indus Plain, across parts of north-western and western Pakistan. It forms an important physical boundary between highlands and lower land.

Mountain areas are not all the same height or shape. Some have sharp peaks, while others have rounded hills, broad valleys or lower passes. Use the map and photograph supplied in a question to identify the particular feature.

5. Potwar Plateau and Salt Range

The Potwar Plateau is an upland region between the northern Punjab area and the Salt Range. It is not a perfectly flat table. Much of it is dissected by streams and erosion, creating uneven slopes, ravines and gullies.

A gully is a small, steep-sided channel cut into soil by running water. Many gullies together can create deeply dissected or badland-like terrain.

The Salt Range lies to the south of the Potwar region. It contains important mineral resources, including rock salt, gypsum and limestone. The Khewra salt mine is a well-known example.

How to describe the Potwar Plateau

It is an elevated area with an uneven surface, dissected by numerous streams and gullies. Its land is generally more rugged than the flat Indus Plain.

6. Balochistan Plateau

The Balochistan Plateau occupies much of western and south-western Pakistan. It consists of elevated basins, mountain ranges and dry valleys rather than one perfectly flat surface.

Much of the plateau is arid or semi-arid. Water is limited in many places, and some streams flow only after rainfall. In inland basins, water may collect in a depression and evaporate instead of reaching the sea.

A basin is a low area surrounded by higher ground. A closed drainage basin is one where water does not flow out to the ocean through a permanent river.

Mountain groups and ranges in and around the plateau include the Toba Kakar, Chagai and Kirthar areas. The syllabus specifically names the Balochistan Plateau, but you should use your prescribed map to learn the exact locations of the features required for your course.

7. The Indus Plain

The Indus Plain is a large lowland region formed mainly by river deposits. It stretches through Punjab and Sindh and is one of Pakistan's most important areas for agriculture, settlement, industry and transport.

Much of the plain has gentle slopes and relatively low relief. The Indus and its tributaries have deposited sediment over long periods, producing extensive areas of alluvial soil.

Alluvium is material such as sand, silt and clay transported and deposited by rivers. New alluvium can replenish soil nutrients, although the value of a deposit depends on its composition and local conditions.

Different surfaces of a river plain

Active floodplain

Low land close to a river channel that can be flooded relatively frequently. Floodwater may spread sediment across the surface. It is sometimes called khaddar in South Asian geographical terminology.

Older floodplain

An older surface that is generally higher or farther from the present channel and is flooded less often than the active floodplain. It may contain abandoned channels and meander scars.

Doab

A doab is an area of land lying between two rivers. It may contain different levels of floodplain and older alluvial surfaces. The name is made from words meaning two and water.

8. The deserts

A desert is a region where rainfall is very low or unreliable compared with the amount of water lost through evaporation and plant use. Deserts can be hot or cold. Not every desert is entirely covered by sand.

Thar Desert

The Thar Desert lies in south-eastern Pakistan, mainly in Sindh, close to the Indian border. It contains sandy areas, dunes and dry conditions. Its rainfall is generally low and unreliable.

Thal Desert

The Thal Desert lies in Punjab, between the Indus and Jhelum river systems. It contains sandy surfaces and dry conditions, although parts have been brought under irrigation and cultivation.

Kharan Desert

The Kharan Desert lies in western Balochistan. It is an inland arid region where water is scarce and settlements are more widely spaced than in the irrigated Indus Plain.

Exam requirement

For the 2027 syllabus, you must use suitable vocabulary to describe mountains, plateaux, floodplains and deserts, and identify named features on maps, photographs and drawings. You do not need to learn the geological formation of Pakistan's natural topography for this section.

LESSON 05 / RIVERS AND DRAINAGE

Pakistan's drainage system

Drainage describes how water moves across the land through rivers and streams. Pakistan's main river network is centred on the Indus.

1. What is a drainage system?

A drainage system is the network of rivers and smaller streams that collects and carries water through an area. The water may come from rainfall, melting snow, melting glaciers or groundwater.

Source

The starting point of a river. It may be a spring, lake, glacier or area of high ground where streams begin.

Tributary

A smaller river or stream that flows into a larger river rather than directly into the sea.

Confluence

The point where two rivers or streams meet.

River channel

The route or bed through which river water normally flows.

Mouth

The place where a river enters the sea, lake or another larger body of water.

Watershed

A ridge or area of higher land that separates neighbouring drainage basins.

2. The Indus River

The Indus River is the main river of Pakistan's drainage and irrigation system. It originates in the Tibetan Plateau region, flows through the high mountains and then travels south through Pakistan before reaching the Arabian Sea.

In the north, the river receives water from rainfall, snowmelt and glacier melt. Farther south, it forms a major river corridor through the Indus Plain.

The river is important because it supplies water for irrigation, supports settlements and industry, and is linked to reservoirs, barrages and canals.

5. Why drainage matters

River systems provide water for farming, household use and industrial processes. They also support ecosystems and can supply water to reservoirs used for hydroelectric power.

However, river water is not always available in the same quantity throughout the year. Rainfall, snowmelt, glacier melt, droughts and floods all affect river flow.

Water also moves through canals and other irrigation channels. A canal is a human-made waterway used to transport water, often for irrigation. A barrage is a structure built across a river to control and raise the water level so that it can be diverted into canals.

Important distinction

Do not say that every river named in the syllabus directly flows into the Arabian Sea. Some are tributaries of other rivers. The Hab and Dasht are examples of south-western coastal rivers, while the Indus is the main river flowing to the Arabian Sea.

LESSON 06 / PEOPLE AND THE ENVIRONMENT

How topography affects human activities

Land is not equally suitable for every activity. The height, slope, soil, water supply and accessibility of an area influence how people live and work.

1. Why does topography affect people?

People adapt their activities to the physical environment. A flat, fertile plain is usually easier to cultivate with large machines than a steep mountain slope. A mountain valley may provide a route through high terrain, while a desert may have very limited water for settlements.

The key idea is that physical conditions create opportunities and limitations. They do not determine everything: technology, investment, transport, irrigation and government planning can change how land is used.

2. Agriculture: flat land and steep slopes

Flat land — opportunities

  • Large fields are easier to establish on relatively flat surfaces.
  • Tractors and other machinery can operate more easily than on steep slopes.
  • Canals and irrigation channels are generally easier to construct on gentle gradients.
  • Alluvial soils in many parts of the Indus Plain can be productive when water and soil conditions are suitable.

Steep land — limitations

  • Machinery is difficult and sometimes unsafe to use on steep slopes.
  • Soil may be shallow, rocky or easily eroded.
  • Flat land for large fields is limited in narrow valleys.
  • Cold temperatures and snow at high elevations can shorten the growing season.

3. How people farm in mountain regions

In some mountain valleys, people use terracing. Terraces are step-like, relatively level surfaces cut into a slope. They make cultivation possible on land that would otherwise be too steep for ordinary fields.

Terraces can slow surface runoff and reduce the speed at which water removes soil. They require labour to construct and maintain, and they do not remove all risks from heavy rain or landslides.

Mountain communities may also grow fruit, keep livestock or use valley floors where land is more accessible. The type of farming depends on altitude, water, soil and local climate.

5. Settlements and industry

Settlements often develop where water, flatter land, jobs and transport are available. The Indus Plain contains large cities and extensive farming areas because much of the terrain is relatively accessible.

Mountain settlements may be concentrated along valley floors or transport routes. However, narrow valleys can limit space for housing, industry and roads. In desert regions, water availability is a major factor affecting settlement size and location.

Industries also need reliable water supplies, transport links and suitable land. A factory built on an inaccessible mountain slope may face greater construction and transport costs than one located on a suitable plain.

Build developed explanations

Instead of writing "mountains make transport hard", explain the chain: steep slopes require winding roads and additional engineering structures; this increases construction and maintenance costs; therefore, routes may be slower and less reliable.

LESSON 07 / CLIMATE FOUNDATIONS

Understanding Pakistan's climate

Climate is more than whether a place is hot or cold. It includes long-term patterns of temperature, rainfall, wind and seasonal changes.

1. Weather versus climate

Weather

Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular time and place. It includes temperature, rainfall, cloud cover and wind.

Example: Karachi is experiencing a hot, humid afternoon with strong sea breezes.

Climate

Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a region over a long period, generally measured using many years of observations.

Example: A region may have hot summers, cool winters and low annual rainfall.

2. Temperature

Temperature measures how hot or cold the air is. It is commonly measured in degrees Celsius (°C).

Temperature differs between places because of factors such as latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, cloud cover and the direction of prevailing winds.

Maximum temperature is the highest temperature recorded during a period, while minimum temperature is the lowest.

3. Rainfall and precipitation

Rainfall is the amount of rain received at a place over a period of time. It is usually measured in millimetres (mm).

Precipitation is the wider term for water falling from the atmosphere to the Earth's surface. It includes rain, snow, sleet and hail.

Annual rainfall is the total rainfall received over a year. Seasonal distribution matters too: two places can receive similar annual totals but have very different rainfall patterns across the year.

4. What controls temperature and rainfall?

Latitude

Latitude affects the angle at which sunlight reaches the Earth's surface and the seasonal length of daylight. Places closer to the Equator generally receive more direct solar energy throughout the year.

Altitude

Altitude means height above sea level. Air temperature generally decreases with increasing altitude, so high mountain areas are usually colder than lowland areas at similar latitudes.

Distance from the sea

The sea heats and cools more slowly than land. Coastal places often have a smaller annual temperature range, while inland places can experience hotter summers and colder winters.

Relief and mountain barriers

Mountains force moist air to rise. Rising air cools and may produce rain on the windward side. Areas on the leeward side can receive less rainfall and may lie in a rain shadow.

Prevailing winds

Prevailing winds are the winds that usually blow from a particular direction in a region. They can carry moist ocean air or dry continental air into a place.

Pressure systems

Low-pressure systems often involve rising air and unsettled weather. High-pressure systems are more commonly associated with sinking air and settled conditions, although local weather can be more complex.

5. Latitude, longitude and day length

The Earth is tilted on its axis. As it moves around the Sun, the Northern and Southern Hemispheres receive different amounts of daylight and solar energy at different times of year.

Places at higher latitudes generally experience a greater seasonal change in day length than places nearer the Equator. Pakistan lies in the Northern Hemisphere, so summer days are longer than winter days.

The northern parts of Pakistan are at higher latitudes than the southern parts. Therefore, the seasonal difference in day length is generally greater in the north.

Longitude has a different role. It helps establish east–west position and is related to local solar time. A place farther east experiences local midday earlier than a place farther west. Longitude by itself does not make a place hotter or colder.

Avoid this common mistake

Do not write that longitude directly controls temperature in the same way as latitude. Latitude affects the angle of incoming sunlight and seasonal daylight. Longitude mainly identifies east–west position and local solar time. Climate differences from east to west need to be explained using actual factors such as distance from the sea, altitude, relief and weather systems.

6. Why is Pakistan's climate varied?

Pakistan is not one uniform climate region. The north contains high mountains, the west includes extensive dry highlands and plateaux, the Indus Plain has hot summers, and the southern coast experiences some moderation from the Arabian Sea.

Rainfall also varies. Some northern and north-eastern areas receive substantial monsoon or winter precipitation, while parts of western and south-western Pakistan are much drier.

LESSON 08 / RAINFALL SYSTEMS

How does Pakistan receive rain?

Pakistan receives rainfall from different weather systems. The syllabus places particular emphasis on the monsoon and asks you to understand its causes.

1. The summer monsoon

A monsoon is a seasonal pattern of winds that changes direction between seasons. In South Asia, the summer monsoon brings moist winds from the surrounding ocean areas towards the heated land.

Pakistan's main summer monsoon period is generally from July to September, although the precise start, strength, timing and retreat vary from year to year.

Why does the summer monsoon develop?

The land heats up more quickly than the sea during the summer. Strong heating over the South Asian landmass encourages air pressure to fall over the heated land. This creates a pressure difference between land and ocean.

Air moves from areas of relatively higher pressure towards areas of lower pressure. Moist air is drawn inland from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal regions. The winds carry water vapour, which can form clouds and rainfall when air rises and cools.

SUMMER MONSOON — SIMPLIFIED PROCESS 1. SOUTH ASIAN LAND HEATS STRONGLY | V 2. AIR PRESSURE FALLS OVER HOT LAND | V 3. MOIST AIR IS DRAWN IN FROM OCEANS | V 4. AIR RISES, COOLS AND CONDENSES | V 5. CLOUDS DEVELOP AND RAINFALL OCCURS

Why do some places receive more monsoon rain?

Moist winds do not deliver the same rainfall everywhere. The amount of rain depends on the moisture in the air, the strength and direction of the winds, the path of weather systems and the shape of the land.

When moist air is forced to rise over higher land, it cools. If it cools sufficiently, water vapour condenses to form cloud droplets and precipitation. This process can increase rainfall near mountain slopes exposed to moist winds.

Parts of north-eastern Pakistan and the northern foothills can receive significant monsoon rainfall, while many western and south-western areas remain comparatively dry.

Monsoon chain for an exam answer

Summer heating makes the land warmer than the sea → air pressure falls over the land → moist ocean air moves inland → air rises and cools → condensation occurs → rainfall is produced.

2. Western depressions

A depression is a large area of relatively low atmospheric pressure. Western depressions are weather systems that move eastwards from the western regions towards South Asia.

They can bring winter rain to parts of Pakistan and snowfall to the northern and western mountains. They are particularly important because they provide precipitation outside the main summer monsoon period.

In high mountain regions, precipitation may fall as snow rather than rain. Snow and ice can later contribute to river flow when temperatures rise.

3. Convectional rainfall and thunderstorms

Convection is the vertical movement of air caused by differences in temperature and density. When the ground becomes strongly heated, it warms the air above it. Warm air expands, becomes less dense and rises.

As the air rises, it expands and cools. If sufficient water vapour is present, condensation occurs and clouds can develop. Strong convection may produce cumulonimbus clouds, heavy showers, thunderstorms, lightning, gusty winds and sometimes hail.

4. Tropical cyclones

A tropical cyclone is an intense rotating low-pressure weather system that develops over sufficiently warm tropical seas. The North Indian Ocean region, including the Arabian Sea, can experience tropical cyclones.

Cyclones can bring very strong winds, heavy rainfall, high waves and storm surges. A storm surge is an abnormal rise in sea level caused mainly by storm winds and atmospheric pressure. It can cause coastal flooding.

Cyclones are not the same as ordinary monsoon rain. They are more intense organised storms and can create serious hazards along the coast.

LESSON 09 / CLIMATE REGIONS

Arid, semi-arid, humid and highland climates

A climate region is an area with broadly similar patterns of temperature and precipitation. Pakistan contains several different climate environments.

1. Arid regions

Arid means very dry. An arid region receives little rainfall compared with the amount of water lost through evaporation and plant use.

Rainfall is often unreliable, and long dry periods can occur. Water supplies may be limited, vegetation is often sparse, and farming can be difficult without irrigation or access to groundwater.

Much of western and south-western Pakistan has arid conditions. Dry areas also occur in parts of the south and south-east, including desert regions.

2. Semi-arid regions

Semi-arid means partly or moderately dry. These areas usually receive more rainfall than arid regions, but rainfall can still be unreliable and may not be sufficient for all farming without additional water.

Semi-arid areas often have a short or uncertain growing season. Drought can cause serious problems because crops and livestock depend on rainfall that may fail.

Parts of the Indus Plain and areas bordering drier regions experience semi-arid conditions, but the exact boundaries depend on the climate classification being used.

3. Humid regions

Humid means that the air contains a relatively high amount of water vapour. In climate classification, humid regions generally receive greater precipitation than arid and semi-arid regions.

Parts of northern and north-eastern Pakistan receive higher rainfall because of the influence of monsoon systems, winter precipitation and mountain relief. The exact amount of rainfall varies between locations and from year to year.

Humid conditions can support more natural vegetation and farming opportunities, although heavy rainfall may also increase flood and soil erosion risks.

4. Highland regions

Highland climate refers to the climate of elevated areas such as mountains and plateaux. Temperature generally decreases with altitude, so high mountain areas can be much colder than nearby lowlands.

Higher areas may experience long cold winters, snowfall and ice, while lower valleys can have milder conditions. Rainfall varies depending on exposure to moist winds and the position of mountain ranges.

The northern mountains and some western highlands contain strong variations in climate over short distances because elevation changes rapidly.

LESSON 10 / CLIMATE AND PEOPLE

How climate affects everyday life and the economy

Climate creates both opportunities and problems. Its effects can be seen in farming, industry, transport, settlements, water supplies and people's daily routines.

1. Low temperature, ice and snow in the mountains

High mountain areas experience low temperatures, especially during winter. Snow and ice affect how people travel, farm, work and access services.

Transport problems

Snow and ice can make roads slippery or blocked. Heavy snowfall may close mountain passes and interrupt road links between valleys and settlements.

Effects on farming

Cold conditions can shorten the growing season. Some crops cannot grow during the coldest months, while livestock may need stored fodder and sheltered spaces.

Water supply

Frozen pipes and water sources can create difficulties. However, snow and glacial melt also contribute to river flows when temperatures rise.

Tourism and work

Snow-covered scenery can attract tourists and create work in hospitality and guiding. However, severe weather can reduce access and disrupt businesses.

2. Rainstorms and flooding

Flooding occurs when water covers land that is normally dry. It may happen when rivers overflow their banks, when intense rainfall overwhelms drainage systems, or when water flows rapidly downhill.

Floods can affect agricultural areas, industrial sites, roads, bridges and railway lines. The severity of the effect depends on the amount of water, flood duration, land use, drainage and the protection available.

Agriculture

Floodwater can submerge crops, wash away seeds, damage fields, drown livestock and delay planting or harvesting. It may also deposit fresh sediment and recharge some water sources, but these possible benefits do not cancel out severe flood damage.

Industry

Factories may be damaged or forced to stop production if buildings, machinery, storage areas or access roads are flooded. Workers may be unable to reach workplaces, and supply deliveries may be delayed.

Communications and transport

Floods can damage roads, bridges and railway infrastructure. This can isolate settlements, interrupt trade and delay emergency services.

Health and daily life

Floods may contaminate drinking water, damage homes and disrupt electricity and sanitation. Clean-water shortages and poor sanitation can increase the risk of disease.

3. Drought and water shortages

Drought is a prolonged period of unusually low water availability, often associated with a lack of rainfall. A drought can develop gradually and may last for months or longer.

Water shortage means that available water is insufficient to meet demand. It may result from drought, overuse of groundwater, poor storage, pollution or weak water management.

Effects on agriculture

Less water is available for irrigation, crops may fail and yields may fall. Pasture becomes scarce, affecting livestock and the income of farming households.

Effects on industry

Industries that need water for cleaning, cooling, processing or manufacturing may reduce production when supplies are restricted.

Effects on people

Communities may face restrictions on household water use. People may have to travel farther to collect water, and water quality may worsen when supplies are low.

Effects on energy

Low river flows and reduced reservoir levels can limit hydroelectric generation. The overall electricity supply also depends on other energy sources and the wider power system.

4. Managing climate-related problems

Possible responses include improved flood forecasting, early-warning systems, better drainage, stronger infrastructure, efficient irrigation, water storage, groundwater monitoring and drought-resistant farming practices.

These measures have costs and limitations. For example, dams can store water but involve construction costs, environmental impacts and the need for careful management. No single response removes every risk.

5. Build a cause-and-effect explanation

Example: drought and agriculture

Point: Drought can reduce agricultural production.

Development: A prolonged lack of rainfall reduces water available for crops. Irrigation supplies may also become limited as river flows and reservoirs fall.

Consequence: Crops may grow poorly or fail, reducing yields and farmers' incomes. This can also reduce the amount of food available in local markets.

LESSON 11 / EXAM SKILLS

Map reading and geographical answers

Knowing the facts is only one part of geography. You must also interpret maps, recognise physical features and express geographical information accurately.

1. How to locate a coordinate

When using a map with latitude and longitude, read both directions carefully. Latitude tells you north or south. Longitude tells you east or west.

Find the required latitude line first. Then find the longitude line. The point where the two lines cross gives the approximate location.

2. How to describe the position of a place

A strong location description uses more than a vague direction. Include the general part of the country, a nearby feature and a direction where possible.

Weak description

Gwadar is in the south.

More precise description

Gwadar is in south-western Pakistan, in Balochistan, on the Arabian Sea coast.

3. How to identify landforms in photographs

Mountain

Look for high land rising above surrounding areas, steep slopes, peaks and valleys.

Plateau

Look for elevated land with a relatively broad, flat or gently rolling upper surface. The sides may be steep.

Plain

Look for an extensive area of low, relatively flat or gently undulating land.

Floodplain

Look for low-lying land beside a river channel. It may be flat and contain old channels or meander features.

Desert

Look for sparse vegetation, dry ground and possibly sand dunes or exposed rock. Remember that deserts are not always entirely sandy.

River valley

Look for a lower corridor between higher areas, often with a stream or river running along its floor.

A developed geographical explanation usually connects a point to a reason and then to its effect.

Example: why can mountain roads be expensive?

Consequence: More engineering work, labour and materials are needed, increasing construction and maintenance costs.
