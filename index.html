1(c) Natural topography & drainage

Pakistan's major physical regions include the northern mountains, western mountains, Potwar Plateau and Salt Range, Balochistan Plateau, Indus Plain and deserts.

Northern mountains

Three major ranges: Himalayas, Karakoram and Hindu Kush.

  • K2: 8,611 m, Karakoram.
  • Nanga Parbat: 8,126 m, Himalayas.
  • Tirich Mir: about 7,708 m, Hindu Kush (the supplied 7,690 m is outdated/approximate; verify your textbook's rounded figure).
  • Features include gorges, cirques, scree slopes and glaciers such as Siachen, Baltoro and Batura.
  • Passes include Khunjerab, Babusar and Lowari (road/tunnel).

Western mountains

  • Safed Koh: Kabul and Kurram drainage; Khyber Pass is in the wider north-western mountain corridor.
  • Waziristan Hills: Tochi and Gomal routes/passes.
  • Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges: separate parts of the Balochistan Plateau from the Indus Plain; Bolan Pass provides a route through the Sulaiman area.

Plateaux: Potwar and Balochistan

Potwar Plateau & Salt Range

  • Supplied elevation: roughly 300–600 m (local variation exists).
  • Badland topography: deeply dissected ravines, gullies and ridges caused by erosion; loess occurs in parts.
  • Drainage: Soan River is a major river.
  • Salt Range: rock salt (Khewra), gypsum and limestone.

Balochistan Plateau

  • Supplied elevation: roughly 600–1,000 m in many areas; relief varies widely.
  • Arid plateau with mountain ranges including Chagai, Toba Kakar and Makran.
  • Inland drainage: some streams terminate in closed basins instead of reaching the sea.
  • Hamuns: seasonal/ephemeral lakes or salt flats, e.g. Hamun-i-Mashkel.
  • Coastal rivers include Hab, Porali, Hingol and Dasht; drainage characteristics vary.

1. Active flood plain (Khaddar)

Low ground beside the channel, flooded frequently in many areas and receiving fresh alluvium. Braided channels and shifting banks may occur.

2. Old flood plain

Higher/older floodplain surfaces flooded less often, especially during larger floods. May show abandoned channels, oxbow lakes and meander scars.

3. Bar / alluvial terrace

Raised surface of older alluvium in a doab (examples include Rechna and Sandal Bars). It is generally less exposed to ordinary floods than the active floodplain, but “completely safe” is too absolute: flood risk depends on location, river behaviour and protection.

4. Cuestas

Asymmetric ridges formed in gently dipping rock layers. Rohri and Ganjo Takkar are examples in the lower Indus region. Their rock can provide stable sites, but barrage location and foundations require engineering assessment.

1(d) Climate mechanisms

The supplied notes identify four precipitation mechanisms. Their timing and effects are broad patterns; regional and year-to-year variation matters.

1. Summer monsoon (usually July–September)

Seasonal heating helps establish low pressure over the land, drawing moist air from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Orographic uplift can produce heavy rainfall along northern foothills. Do not claim all of Pakistan receives heavy monsoon rain.

2. Western depressions (mainly winter)

Mid-latitude weather systems move east from the Mediterranean/western regions across Iran and Afghanistan. They bring winter precipitation to western/northern Pakistan, including rain and mountain snowfall.

3. Convectional rain / thunderstorms

Strong surface heating makes warm air rise; cooling and condensation can form cumulonimbus clouds, thunderstorms, gusts and sometimes hail. Season and location vary.

4. Tropical cyclones

Occasional cyclones form over the Arabian Sea, especially during pre- and post-monsoon periods. Coastal hazards include strong winds, storm surge and heavy rain; exact timing and impact are not predictable from this general note.

Climatic zones

These four labels are a simplified classroom grouping, not the only possible climate classification. Use your prescribed textbook's names and boundaries.

1. Highland

Northern and western mountains

  • Long cold winters, often with snow at higher elevations.
  • Shorter, generally milder summers.
  • Rainfall varies greatly: Murree is wetter; Gilgit lies in a rain-shadow setting and is much drier.

2. Lowland

Indus Plain

  • Continental conditions: very hot summers and cooler winters.
  • Upper Punjab generally receives more rainfall than lower Sindh.
  • Rainfall and temperature figures depend on location and period of record.

3. Coastal / maritime

Arabian Sea coast, including Karachi and Gwadar

  • Sea influence moderates temperatures and often reduces daily/annual temperature range.
  • Humid conditions and sea breezes occur, especially in warm months.
  • Rainfall is generally low and variable; exact totals should be sourced by station.

4. Arid

Deserts and dry parts of Balochistan

  • Very low and unreliable rainfall.
  • Hot days and cooler/cold nights can create a large daily temperature range.
  • Dry winds and dust may occur; named wind duration claims need a source.

Climate: economy and daily life

Cold mountain winters

  • Transhumance: seasonal movement of livestock between high summer pastures and lower winter areas.
  • Snow/ice may freeze soil, block roads and disrupt water pipes or farming.
  • Some households may shift to indoor crafts such as carpet-making, embroidery and woodwork.
  • Winter tourism may support local economies where facilities and access permit.

Rainstorms and floods

  • Floods can damage crops, livestock, bridges, roads and railway infrastructure.
  • Standing contaminated water can increase risk of waterborne disease and mosquito-borne illness; outbreaks depend on sanitation and public-health response.
  • Floodwater may recharge groundwater, fill reservoirs and deposit fertile silt, but these benefits are not guaranteed and can be outweighed by damage.

Drought

  • Water and pasture shortages can reduce crop yields and harm livestock, especially in arid districts.
  • Wind erosion and land degradation may contribute to desertification where land is vulnerable.
  • People may migrate temporarily or permanently for work and water; patterns vary.
  • Low reservoir inflows can reduce hydropower generation, but load-shedding also depends on the wider electricity system.

Searchable glossary

Alluvium

Material such as silt, sand and clay deposited by rivers or floodwater.

Doab

Land between two rivers; also called an interfluve.

Bar / alluvial terrace

A raised surface of older alluvial deposits within a river plain or doab; flood exposure varies.

Active floodplain (Khaddar)

Low river-adjacent land that is frequently flooded and may receive fresh sediment.

Hamun

A shallow lake or depression, often seasonal and saline, in an inland drainage basin.

Monsoon

A seasonal wind pattern, often involving a seasonal reversal, associated with changes in rainfall.

Western depression

A mid-latitude low-pressure weather system moving eastward and bringing precipitation to Pakistan.

Transhumance

Seasonal movement of people and livestock between different grazing areas, often highlands and lowlands.

Badland topography

Heavily eroded terrain with gullies, ravines and sharp ridges, often with sparse vegetation.

Cuesta

An asymmetrical ridge formed by gently dipping rock layers, with a steep scarp slope and a gentler dip slope.

Oxbow lake

A crescent-shaped lake formed when a meander loop is cut off from the river channel.

Rain shadow

A relatively dry area on the leeward side of a mountain range after air loses moisture on the windward side.

Diurnal temperature range

The difference between the day's maximum and minimum temperature.

Orographic rainfall

Rain caused when moist air is forced to rise over higher land, cools and condenses.

Storm surge

An abnormal rise in sea level caused mainly by a storm's winds and pressure, potentially flooding coasts.
